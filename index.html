Uyat · shame

Does sex change what love means?

PLS 210 · Research Design Part 1 · Fall 2026 · Nazarbayev University · Pseudonym: X

The puzzle

Research question

  • 2024 pilot: 20 male NU students. Sex makes love stronger, but more experience means less “amorousness.”
  • I only asked men.

How do male and female NU undergraduates describe the link between sexual experience and love in the 2026–2027 academic year?

EmpiricalHow they describe it, not how they should live
ScopedNU undergraduates, by gender, 2026–2027
AnswerableThrough interviews
Where I stand

Positioning: interpretivist

This study is interpretivist: it tries to understand what sex and love mean to students themselves, rather than to measure them (Schwartz-Shea & Yanow, 2012).

Words like “love” and “amorousness” have no fixed definition, and my pilot participants built their own, so a 1–10 survey would force my categories on them.

I compare the stories men and women tell, not whether gender causes different feelings.

Because shame shapes what people admit, and findings on double standards depend on how people are asked (Crawford & Popp, 2003), private open-ended interviews suit this topic best.

What this adds

Contribution

Research on sexuality in Kazakhstan mostly looks at sex education, parents and large-scale attitudes (Kabatova, 2022; Dall’Agnola & Thibault, 2021).

Research on sexual double standards and campus sexuality comes mostly from the United States (Crawford & Popp, 2003; Hamilton & Armstrong, 2009).

No one has asked NU men and women how they link sexual experience and love, in their own words.

NU, an English-speaking international university full of students raised with uyat, is a good place to see where local norms meet global scripts.

What others found

Literature review

Simon and Gagnon (1986) show that sex is learned like a script, at cultural, interpersonal and intrapsychic levels, which gives my study its lens.

Crawford and Popp (2003) find the sexual double standard still exists but is subtle and depends on context, and Hamilton and Armstrong (2009) show US college women caught in “double binds” in both hookups and relationships, so comparing genders is worthwhile.

In Central Asia, Kabatova (2022) shows that uyat silences talk about sex, especially for girls, and Zhanabayeva (2018) finds dating accepted but premarital sex condemned for women, which tells me what to listen for.

Dall’Agnola and Thibault (2021) find young urban Kazakhs more exposed online to content that challenges marriage norms, suggesting NU students live between local and global scripts.

Tension: the US campus pushes casual sex. In Central Asia, sex puts women’s honour at risk.

NU sits between the two.

My lens

Analytical framework

Gendered sexual scripts under uyat. These are sensitising concepts, not fixed codes.

CulturalThe shared rules society teaches, what “everyone knows” (e.g. “sex should come with love”).
InterpersonalHow students apply or bend those rules with a real partner.
IntrapsychicHow they explain their own desires and feelings to themselves, like “amorousness.”
Uyat (filter)Doesn’t decide what students believe, but what they feel able to say aloud, to whom, and in which language.

Expectations

  • Men: experience = maturity, or less amorousness.
  • Women: trust, reputation, marriage. They’ll speak more carefully.
  • Both: a gap between their own beliefs and what their family expects.
  • Jokes, silences and language switching show where uyat works.

Positionality

Insider with men

Rapport, but they may perform masculinity.

Outsider with women

It’s uyat to talk about sex with a man.

Response: a female co-interviewer option, written answers, cameras off, and I’ll report the effect.
